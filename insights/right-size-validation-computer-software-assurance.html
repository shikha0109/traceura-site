How to right-size validation with FDA's Computer Software Assurance approach

A practical guide for quality and CSV teams: what the final guidance says, how to scale validation effort to risk, and a checklist for your next change.

The Traceura team · 19 November 2026 · 7-minute read

In short: spend validation effort where a software failure could actually harm product quality or patient safety, test lower-risk functions more lightly, and write down why you decided each one. The decision and its rationale are what an inspector will ask about, not the number of screenshots.

What FDA published, and who it applies to

In September 2025 FDA finalised its guidance Computer Software Assurance for Production and Quality System Software. It was issued by FDA's device and biologics centres (CDRH and CBER) and is written for medical device manufacturers under 21 CFR Part 820. It supplements FDA's 2002 General Principles of Software Validation and replaces that document's section on validating automated process equipment and quality system software.

For pharmaceutical manufacturers the guidance is not formally binding. Many pharma quality teams still use it as a reference, because its risk-based, intended-use thinking closely matches ISPE's GAMP 5 (Second Edition). The practical message carries across: validation rigor should follow risk, not habit.

The core idea: effort follows risk to intended use

Traditional computer system validation often applied the same heavy, scripted approach to every function. Computer Software Assurance asks a simpler question first: if this feature failed, could it lead to a quality problem that foreseeably compromises safety? The guidance calls that high process risk. Everything else is not high process risk, and can be assured with lighter methods. Teams are free to use more than two levels if that suits their procedures.

Four steps to right-size a validation decision

1. State the intended use

Be specific about what the software, or the changed feature, is used for in your process. "LIMS" is not an intended use; "calculates and reports stability results used for batch release" is.

2. Decide the risk

For each feature, ask whether a failure could foreseeably compromise product quality or patient safety. Consider what other controls would catch the problem, such as a second-person review or a downstream check.

3. Choose assurance activities to match

Process riskTypical assurance activities
HighMore rigorous, traceable testing, such as scripted tests with documented expected results
Not highUnscripted testing, such as scenario testing, error-guessing or exploratory testing, recorded concisely
EitherReuse credible supplier evidence (certifications, development and security practices, data integrity controls) instead of repeating it

The guidance does not mandate a specific method for each risk level. The point is that the choice is deliberate and proportionate.

4. Keep the right record

The record should show the intended use, the risk determination, what was tested, who tested it and when, the results and any issues, and the conclusion. The guidance encourages digital records such as system logs and audit trails over paper and screenshots.

A worked example (fictional)

A laboratory system receives a vendor update with two changes:

  • Change A adds a new layout option to an internal workload report. A failure would be inconvenient but would not affect any quality decision. Not high process risk: a short exploratory check, recorded in a few lines, is proportionate.
  • Change B alters how a result used in batch release is calculated. A failure could lead to a wrong release decision. High process risk: scripted tests against known expected values, with traceable results.

Same release, two very different scopes. Both are defensible, because the reasoning for each is written down.

Where teams usually get stuck

  • Inconsistent risk calls. Two reviewers, or two sites, judge the same kind of change differently. Inspectors notice.
  • Rationale that isn't recorded. The decision was sound, but the reason lives in someone's head or an email thread.
  • Old habits. Teams keep scripting everything because "that's what we did last time", which costs time without reducing risk.
  • Supplier evidence ignored. Vendor testing and certifications are repeated instead of assessed and reused.

Checklist for your next change

  • Is the intended use of each changed feature written in one specific sentence?
  • Has each feature been judged high or not high process risk, with a one-line reason?
  • Do the assurance activities match the risk, and is that choice explained?
  • Has credible supplier evidence been assessed and reused where appropriate?
  • Does the record show what was tested, by whom, when, the results and the conclusion?
  • Would a similar change at another site be scoped the same way?
