What the EU's draft Annex 22 means for AI in GMP decisions

Annex 22 is not law yet, but its direction is clear. Here is what the draft says, what it expects when AI touches GMP, and what quality teams can sensibly do now.

The Traceura team · 19 November 2026 · 7-minute read

In short: the draft allows fixed, deterministic AI models in critical GMP applications if they are properly validated. It keeps self-learning models, generative AI and large language models out of critical decisions, and allows them in non-critical work only with a qualified person in the loop. Whatever the final text says, documented human oversight is where this is heading.

Where things stand

The European Commission and EMA published the draft of a new GMP Annex 22 on artificial intelligence in July 2025, alongside drafts of a revised Annex 11 (computerised systems) and Chapter 4 (documentation). Public consultation ran from 7 July to 7 October 2025 and drew a large number of comments. EMA held an expert workshop in mid-2026, and its work plan targets final text by the end of 2026.

As of this article, Annex 22 is still a draft. It is not part of EudraLex Volume 4 and is not enforceable. The final wording may change, especially on generative AI. Treat what follows as the direction of travel, not settled rules.

What is in scope, and what is not

Type of AICritical GMP use (direct impact on product quality, patient safety or data integrity)Non-critical use
Static, deterministic models (parameters fixed in use)Allowed, if validatedAllowed
Dynamic models that keep learning in useNot allowed in the draftAllowed with oversight
Generative AI and large language modelsNot allowed in the draftAllowed, with a qualified person reviewing outputs and taking responsibility

In practice, an AI tool that drafts a deviation summary for a person to review sits very differently from one that decides whether a batch passes.

What the draft expects for AI in critical use

  • A documented intended use, approved by a process expert before testing starts, describing the inputs, expected variation and limitations.
  • Acceptance criteria set in advance. The model should perform at least as well as the process it replaces, which means knowing how well the current process performs.
  • Independent test data, kept separate from training data by technical and procedural controls, with a four-eyes principle for anyone who has seen the test set.
  • Explainability: recording which features drove a result, and reviewing that as part of approving test results.
  • Confidence: logging a confidence score where applicable, and considering an "undecided" outcome rather than forcing a low-confidence answer.
  • Change control and monitoring: assessing changes to the model, the system or the process that feeds it, and watching for drift in the input data.

What it means for non-critical uses

Most practical AI uses in quality today are assistive: drafting summaries, finding related records, suggesting where to look. The draft permits these, provided a qualified person reviews the output and remains accountable for what happens next. That responsibility needs to be real and documented, not a rubber stamp.

Five things quality teams can do now

  1. Inventory where AI is already used, including AI features inside existing vendor software, and note what each one does.
  2. Classify each use as critical or non-critical using your own risk framework, and write down why.
  3. Define what human oversight means for each non-critical use: who reviews, what they check, and how their decision is recorded.
  4. Ask suppliers direct questions: Is the model fixed or does it keep learning? How was it tested, and on what data? Can it show why it gave an answer? What happens when it is unsure?
  5. Watch the companion drafts too. The proposed Annex 11 revision is far more detailed than the 2011 version and makes cybersecurity an explicit GMP topic.
